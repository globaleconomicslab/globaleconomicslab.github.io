About

Help architect a new and improved global economic order.

There is no playbook for the new global economic order. We create cutting-edge research and turn it into insights that help policymakers and corporate leaders navigate change and make sound decisions. Led by Founding Director Gita Gopinath, the Lab brings together senior leadership experience in policy and business with a network of leading economists from Harvard and partner institutions, combining directed research, strategic advising, and teaching to help shape what comes next.

Our Mission

The Global Economics Lab has been created to architect a new and improved global economic order.

The global economic order is the set of rules, institutions, and relationships governing international trade, capital flows, and economic policies. It shapes how exchange rates are set, how capital moves, how trade disputes are resolved, and, ultimately, who benefits from an integrated world economy. It is not a fixed architecture: the global economic order has evolved as the balance of economic and political power has shifted.

The current order took shape after the Second World War. At Bretton Woods in 1944, the United States and its allies created the IMF and the World Bank and established a system of pegged exchange rates under American leadership, laying the foundations of a liberal order built for growth and stability. When advanced economies abandoned fixed dollar exchange rates in 1973, many predicted that globalization and multilateralism had peaked. They were proven wrong, as “economic multilateralism adapted and in some respects grew stronger” (Obstfeld, 2024). The decades that followed brought deeper multilateral engagement, the transformation of the GATT into the WTO, and a historic expansion of trade and capital flows, as more countries, including large emerging economies, integrated into the global system.

The results were quite extraordinary. Global GDP grew roughly fourfold between 1980 and 2022, lifting hundreds of millions out of poverty and turning emerging markets into central players in the world economy. But that economic transformation also reshuffled the geopolitical balance and fueled political backlash in many richer countries, including its bandmaster, the United States.

Today, the order that delivered this growth is under acute strain. Tariffs, export controls, sanctions, and intensifying rivalry between the United States and China are displacing decades of rules-based integration, compounded by climate change and technological revolutions in AI and digital money that existing institutions were not designed to handle. Cooperation is faltering just as the world faces a growing set of threats that demand collective action.

We are, in short, at an inflection point comparable to 1944. The task is not to preserve an order that no longer fits today’s realities, nor to let it unravel by default, but to deliberately architect what comes next: a framework that addresses legitimate concerns about unbalanced trade and about economic and national security, while preserving the gains from open trade and finance. That is the mission the Global Economics Lab exists to serve.
